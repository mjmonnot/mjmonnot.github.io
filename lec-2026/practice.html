Try this quarter

Three moves before anyone scores employee text with a language model, plus questions for a vendor and a one-page meaning map you can fill in a staff meeting.

  1. Seal a holdout before the first prompt is edited. Ten percent of comments, locked, not used for examples.
  2. Write the human bar by dimension. Agency and belonging will not share a ceiling. If two trained people cannot see a code, do not buy a model that claims it.
  3. Put leakage and direct-identifier checks in the scoring job. A slide that says “we take privacy seriously” is not a check.

Ask the vendor

  • Against which human pair, on which codes, was agreement computed?
  • What is the positive specific agreement (or F1) on the present codes, not overall accuracy?
  • How many false present calls on cases two humans coded absent?
  • Was the test set used to rewrite the prompt?
  • Does the product report belonging or authenticity? What was the human ceiling on that dimension?
  • If the model is wrong, how does that error enter the dashboard that managers see?

If they answer with a single F1 and a crowd-worker paper, send them Gilardi et al. (2023) and Törnberg (2024) together. The first is their citation. The second is the standard.

A 20-minute meaning map

In a team that is about to adopt a documentation or monitoring tool, list six tasks. For each, mark: whose meaning does it carry (self, other, both, unclear)? Is it on the automation list? If yes, what occasion for meaning disappears, and what is the replacement? This is a discussion device, not a validated instrument. Our registered map did not forecast next-year strain from the 2 × 2. Competence-anchored stories did, in 17 cases, and that lead is exploratory.
