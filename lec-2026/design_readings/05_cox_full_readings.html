1. Interview with Amanda Cox — Graphics Editor at the New York Times (Simply Statistics)

  • - URL: https://simplystatistics.org/posts/2012-06-01-interview-with-amanda-cox-graphics-editor-at-the-new/
  • - Date: June 1, 2012
  • - Read in full: YES — Cox’s own Q&A answers

Notes (what SHE argues in this interview)

  1. 1. She declines the “statistician” label: doesn’t treat uncertainty formally enough; good days the work is “journalism.”
  2. 2. Path: restless first-year UW stats → applied for random things including NYT graphics internship.
  3. 3. News graphics ≠ scientific audience graphs: “feels like homework” is a negative reaction.
  4. 4. Practical differences: don’t assume readers already care; kill jargon; graphics should often stand alone; typography/design = hierarchy and clarity, not cuteness.
  5. 5. Prototypes in R (base points/lines/text); sometimes clean PDFs in Illustrator; web interactivity changes the R stage into proof-of-concept.
  6. 6. “Poor-man’s interactivity”: pagedown through a PDF of charts from a loop.
  7. 7. Topic choice: news value and interestingness; ideally both.
  8. 8. Data availability shapes scope (e.g., county vs precinct election maps in real time).
  9. 9. Turnaround: “how long do we have?”—projects expand to fill time (hours to weeks); desk proud of one-day breaking work (often less data-heavy).
  10. 10. Annotation layer is critical: annotate example points so readers learn forms by example; words should highlight the relevant pattern or an expert interpretation—not “here is some data.”
  11. 11. Likes sliders (suggest beginning–middle–end even when not time) and meaningful motion that exploits web vs print.

2. The NYT’s Amanda Cox on Winning the Internet (OpenNews / Source report of OpenVis Conf keynote)

  • - URL: https://source.opennews.org/articles/nyts-amanda-cox-wins-internet/
  • - Date: May 17, 2013
  • - Author of page: Erin Kissane (conference report)
  • - Read in full: YES — detailed secondhand write-up of Cox’s OpenVis opening keynote (not a word-for-word transcript)

Notes (arguments attributed to Cox in THIS report)

  1. 1. Question: what makes some Times graphics “better,” and which kinds of better matter?
  2. 2. Social shares ≈ batting average: imperfect quality metric, not uncorrelated with some quality.
  3. 3. Initial guesses for “winning the internet”: surprise; illusory comprehensiveness; goofy comparisons; clear takeaway.
  4. 4. Then she manually coded top ~400 graphics on many traits (surprise.reveal, emotional/atmospheric, useful, development.really.hard, takeaway, etc.) and graphed results.
  5. 5. Empirical top traits for social success: hard development; breaking-news-adjacent; useful; explicitly emotional/atmospheric; surprise reveal; comprehensive.
  6. 6. Difficult topics (war, climate, etc.) did worst; blunt “takeaway” pieces also underperformed as a class.
  7. 7. Signaling importance: use scarce resources—print “good page” placement vs web scarce developer hours.
  8. 8. Two “wows” (via Jonathan Harris / Ed Ruscha): spectacle wow vs intimate connection wow; prefer “Huh? Wow!” over “Wow! Huh?”
  9. 9. Design-as-empathy realization: not mainly whitespace—but helping readers understand and feel.
  10. 10. Little templating—“the best journalism isn’t a mad lib”; craft to the data’s unique shape; abstract frameworks OK.
  11. 11. Transparent versioning (GitHub) so remote colleagues see visual evolution.
  12. 12. Sampling/randomness on web (e.g., county political “wind” map) can show “all at once” differently than print’s forced emphasis.
  13. 13. Closing challenge: tools hide things—“What are you skipping because it doesn’t come for free in your tools?” Goal: find what she’s missing because she thinks differently.

3. Amanda Cox: Making Illustrations Better with an Annotation Layer (Eyeo 2011 attendee notes)

  • - URL: https://www.softwareandart.com/amanda-cox-making-illustrations-better-with-an-annotation-layer/
  • - Date: June 27, 2011 (Isabel Wheeler notes from Eyeo)
  • - Read in full: YES — attendee notes, not official transcript

Notes (Cox claims as recorded here)

  1. 1. Dataviz can learn from illustration: foreground what you care about; leave some detail out; “how many backup singers?”
  2. 2. Mythic over-detailed diagrams (every helicopter bolt) aren’t always necessary; essence often enough—but sometimes detail helps.
  3. 3. Principle: show a pattern, leave some detail (like reporters’ anecdotes).
  4. 4. Examples: Mariano Rivera pitch video (pattern of indistinguishable spin at decision point); tables surfacing what maps hide; comment-sentiment grid; ski-jump video telling viewers when to look at arms/hips.
  5. 5. Add guidance to prevent misinterpretation; avoid scavenger-hunt interfaces.
  6. 6. Online free-for-all (“here’s data, tweet if you find something”) abdicates responsibility.
  7. 7. Need a proper annotation layer—don’t say “here’s an interface, browse forever.”
  8. 8. Sketch on paper through multiple forms until something works.
